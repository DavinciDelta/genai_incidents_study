# Step 5 — Which attack categories stand out against the community vote

Generated by `s05_techniques.py` (`make s05`); do not edit by hand; inputs pinned in `external/PINS.json`. Conventions, 95% CI, pooled rows and the glossary: `out/s01_overview.md` section 0.

Which OWASP LLM Top 10 categories does each population rank higher or lower than the community vote? Two sources: the corpus's OWASP categories and the community vote with its 90% interval as rank-validation reports (20 candidates ranked by respondents; its data rank is Table 1.15). Hand labels from one annotator of incident-rank-validation (a separate project that tests the OWASP ranking) show attacks the list cannot name: a limitation below (Tables 5.4a–b).

Every record stays in: 1,339 ON-AI and 1,460 WITH-AI. Step 4's review could not place 129 ON-AI and 254 WITH-AI records as attacks (no adversary described, or a conventional-exploit record misplaced in WITH-AI); they are counted like the rest, and Tables 5.1–5.2 show how many of each category's records they are. The OWASP categories themselves are the corpus's, set by keyword rules (Limitations of the data); step 4's reviewed labels answer how the attacks happened.

Why this is a limitation:

- *Missing categories.* MCP Tool Interface Exploitation (17 ON-AI records), Cross-Modal Safety Bypass (10 ON-AI records) and Weaponized LLM Abuse (43 WITH-AI records) have no corpus code, so the corpus files those records under other categories (Tables 5.4a–b).
- *Nothing fits.* 14% of ON-AI and 43% of WITH-AI hand-labelled records fit no category. In ON-AI the largest group by corpus attack vector is adversarial-input (8 of 23); in WITH-AI, by step 4's objective, non-consensual / abuse imagery (34 of 74) and no attacker: operator harm or model failure (misplaced record) (17 of 74); 67 of the 74 WITH-AI records are tracker stubs. The list asks for an LLM mechanism, such as input that "alters the model's behavior in ways the operator did not intend", which deepfake abuse imagery, political deepfakes and classifier evaluations lack.
- *Where both use an OWASP category with 10+ hand labels, they mostly agree* (Supply Chain Vulnerabilities 20 of 21; lowest Data and Model Poisoning 13 of 22); on smaller rows less so (Excessive Agency 3 of 9, Unbounded Consumption 4 of 8). The larger gap is the attacks the list cannot name (the proposed rows) and the records that fit nothing.

## Use cases

- *Deployers.* Improper Output Handling's ON-AI +11 is rule output: 238 of 548 records carry a code-injection seed vector, 5% demonstrations (Table 5.1).
- *Researchers.* Prompt Injection, the vote's first, ranks 3rd in ON-AI. The 17 records the annotator filed as MCP Tool Interface Exploitation (the corpus files 14 of them under Supply Chain Vulnerabilities) are a starting sample for that proposed category (Table 5.4a).
- *Framework authors.* 74 of 174 hand-labelled WITH-AI records fit no category, and Weaponized LLM Abuse (43 records) has no corpus code: deepfake abuse imagery and political deepfakes have no LLM mechanism (Table 5.4b).

**Not supported:** prevalence; a corrected Top 10; attack chains; trends (Tables 5.3a–b set one year beside all years, and the record year is often the ingestion year); attribution or exploitation status; statements about how attackers operated.

## Limitations of the data

- The corpus's labels are keyword rules: when a source gives no attack vector, `classify_attack_vector` in the corpus build script takes the first of 41 regular expressions that matches the title and description (for example 'impersonat' → deepfake); records that arrive without OWASP codes get them from the attack vector by a fixed table (36.0% of OWASP codes equal that seed), and ATLAS codes follow from the OWASP codes by lookup (Tables 1.10, 5.1, 5.2).
- Supply Chain Vulnerabilities marks the CVE channel and 604 of 686 cve/ghsa frame records are ON-AI, so pooled comparisons compare channels (Tables 1.10, 2.2).
- One annotator hand-labelled a quota sample: 161 ON-AI and 174 WITH-AI records (Tables 1.14, 5.4a–b).
- Step 2's split is rule-based; Table 2.5 lists its weak spots. Step 4's review of every record found 129 ON-AI (9.6%) and 173 WITH-AI (11.8%) records that describe no adversary, 70 ON-AI records whose entry point fits no value (such as an AI-assisted scam on people) and 81 conventional-exploit records misplaced in WITH-AI; this step keeps all of them in its counts and shows them per category (Tables 4.1, 4.4, 5.1, 5.2).
- Join losses: 77 of 1,200 hand-labelled rows have no current record of their own (Table 1.13).
- Vote–data concordance is weak: weighted κ 0.20 (−0.16 to 0.57) (s01 section 0, 'data rank'; Table 1.15).
- The two OWASP numberings differ on 8 of ten entries, so joins use names (Table 1.0).
- The public record is a small, self-selected sample: only 8.5% of CVE/GHSA records and 24.6% of harm-database records involve an adversary at all, `exploited_in_wild` is set on 14 records, and attacks that are never disclosed, settled privately or seen only in vendor telemetry are absent (Tables 2.2, 2.5).
- The index pools trackers with different units: a CVE is one bug in one product, a harm-database row is one news event, a research row is one paper; counts across them, and the corpus's own merging of 1,340 multi-source records, mix units (s01 section B; Table 1.1).
- Record year, date precision, stubs and empty fields: steps 1, 2 and 4.
