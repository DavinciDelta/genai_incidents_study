# Step 5 — Which attack categories stand out against the community vote

Generated by `s05_techniques.py` (`make s05`); do not edit by hand; inputs pinned in `external/PINS.json`. Conventions, 95% CI, pooled rows and the glossary: `out/s01_overview.md` section 0.

Which OWASP LLM Top 10 categories does each population rank higher or lower than the community vote? Two sources: the corpus's OWASP categories and the community vote with its 90% interval as rank-validation reports (20 candidates ranked by respondents; its data rank is Table 1.15). Hand labels from one annotator of incident-rank-validation (a separate project that tests the OWASP ranking) show attacks the list cannot name: a limitation below (Tables 5.4a–b).

The populations are the cleaned ones from step 4's review: 1,210 ON-AI and 1,206 WITH-AI records. Left out are 129 ON-AI and 254 WITH-AI records that the review found describe no adversary, or are conventional-exploit records misplaced in WITH-AI. The OWASP categories themselves are the corpus's, set by keyword rules (Limitations of the data); step 4's reviewed labels answer how the attacks happened.

## Limitation: hand labels show attacks the OWASP categories cannot name

One annotator of incident-rank-validation hand-labelled 135 ON-AI and 141 WITH-AI records of the cleaned populations. The records were drawn by quota (every record the project's three LLM pre-labellers disagreed on, then 30 unanimous and 30 majority records per category), so the counts describe the sample, not prevalence. Tables 5.4a–b show, for each category the annotator used, how often the corpus files the same record under it.

Why this is a limitation:

- *Missing categories.* MCP Tool Interface Exploitation (17 ON-AI records), Cross-Modal Safety Bypass (10 ON-AI records) and Weaponized LLM Abuse (40 WITH-AI records) have no corpus code, so the corpus files those records under other categories (Tables 5.4a–b).
- *Nothing fits.* 11% of ON-AI and 40% of WITH-AI hand-labelled records fit no category. In ON-AI the largest group by corpus attack vector is adversarial-input (5 of 15); in WITH-AI, by step 4's objective, non-consensual / abuse imagery (34 of 56) and political / influence (13 of 56); 51 of the 56 WITH-AI records are tracker stubs. The list asks for an LLM mechanism, such as input that "alters the model's behavior in ways the operator did not intend", which deepfake abuse imagery, political deepfakes and classifier evaluations lack.
- *Where both use an OWASP category with 10+ hand labels, they mostly agree* (Supply Chain Vulnerabilities 17 of 18; lowest Data and Model Poisoning 12 of 17); on smaller rows less so (Excessive Agency 3 of 8, Sensitive Information Disclosure 1 of 2). The larger gap is the attacks the list cannot name (the proposed rows) and the records that fit nothing.

## Use cases

- *Deployers.* Improper Output Handling's ON-AI +11 is rule output: 201 of 481 records carry a code-injection seed vector, 5% demonstrations (Table 5.1).
- *Researchers.* Prompt Injection, the vote's first, ranks 3rd in ON-AI. The 17 records the annotator filed as MCP Tool Interface Exploitation (the corpus files 14 of them under Supply Chain Vulnerabilities) are a starting sample for that proposed category (Table 5.4a).
- *Framework authors.* 56 of 141 hand-labelled WITH-AI records fit no category, and Weaponized LLM Abuse (40 records) has no corpus code: deepfake abuse imagery and political deepfakes have no LLM mechanism (Table 5.4b).

**Not supported:** prevalence; a corrected Top 10; attack chains; trends (Tables 5.3a–b set one year beside all years, and the record year is often the ingestion year); attribution or exploitation status; statements about how attackers operated.

## Limitations of the data

- The corpus's labels are keyword rules: when a source gives no attack vector, `classify_attack_vector` in the corpus build script takes the first of 41 regular expressions that matches the title and description (for example 'impersonat' → deepfake); records that arrive without OWASP codes get them from the attack vector by a fixed table (36.0% of OWASP codes equal that seed), and ATLAS codes follow from the OWASP codes by lookup (Tables 1.10, 5.1, 5.2).
- Supply Chain Vulnerabilities marks the CVE channel and 604 of 686 cve/ghsa frame records are ON-AI, so pooled comparisons compare channels (Tables 1.10, 2.2).
- One annotator hand-labelled a quota sample: 135 ON-AI and 141 WITH-AI records of the cleaned populations (Tables 1.14, 5.4a–b).
- Step 2's split is rule-based; Table 2.5 lists its weak spots. Step 4's review of every record found 129 ON-AI (9.6%) and 173 WITH-AI (11.8%) records that describe no adversary, 70 ON-AI records whose entry point fits no value (such as an AI-assisted scam on people) and 81 conventional-exploit records misplaced in WITH-AI; this step leaves out the no-adversary and misplaced records (129 ON-AI, 254 WITH-AI) and keeps the rest (Tables 4.1, 4.4).
- Join losses: 77 of 1,200 hand-labelled rows have no current record of their own (Table 1.13).
- Vote–data concordance is weak: weighted κ 0.20 (−0.16 to 0.57) (s01 section 0, 'data rank'; Table 1.15).
- The two OWASP numberings differ on 8 of ten entries, so joins use names (Table 1.0).
- The public record is a small, self-selected sample: only 8.5% of CVE/GHSA records and 24.6% of harm-database records involve an adversary at all, `exploited_in_wild` is set on 14 records, and attacks that are never disclosed, settled privately or seen only in vendor telemetry are absent (Tables 2.2, 2.5).
- The index pools trackers with different units: a CVE is one bug in one product, a harm-database row is one news event, a research row is one paper; counts across them, and the corpus's own merging of 1,340 multi-source records, mix units (s01 section B; Table 1.1).
- Record year, date precision, stubs and empty fields: steps 1, 2 and 4.
