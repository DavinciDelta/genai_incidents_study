# Step 5 — Which attack categories stand out against the expert vote, and which ATLAS mitigations address them

Generated by `s05_techniques.py` (`make s05`); do not edit by hand; inputs pinned in `external/PINS.json`. Conventions, 95% CI, pooled rows and the glossary: `out/s01_overview.md` section 0. Limitations: Table 5.7.

Two questions: which OWASP LLM Top 10 categories stand out in each population against the OWASP community vote, checked against one person's hand labels; and which MITRE ATLAS mitigations address the ON-AI categories, and which labelled techniques have none. Three sources: the corpus's categories are keyword labels, not coded fields; the hand labels are one person's verdicts on a quota sample (161 ON-AI and 174 WITH-AI of the 1,123 hand-labelled records that join; the rest are BOTH, UNRESOLVED or NONE), so no CI and no ranking; the vote and its 90% interval are rank-validation's (its data rank and verdict: Table 1.15). Step 4 answers how the attacks happened and is not repeated; names are rank-validation's entry names, corpus code in brackets.

## Which categories stand out?

Not a prevalence ranking or a corrected Top 10: corpus labels are keyword rules that mark the channel more than the content (Supply Chain Vulnerabilities sits on 98.2% of CVE/GHSA records), the hand-labelled rows are a quota sample, and the vote is 20 candidates ranked by respondents.

*Flag key.* agree = corpus rank within 2 places of the vote rank (the vote spans 20 candidates, the corpus ten coded entries); corpus above / vote above = 3+ places apart; too few records = under 30 corpus records; channel marker = 90%+ of one channel's records carry the code or 90%+ of its labels come from one channel (Supply Chain Vulnerabilities on 98.2% of cve/ghsa records; 98.7% of Misinformation labels from harm-db; 96.0% of Hidden Context Exposure labels from harm-db). Hand-label counts are capped by the 30 + 30 per-entry quota and the all-disagree-rows rule: read them against the corpus column of the same row only.

## Why 43% of the hand-labelled WITH-AI records fit no category

74 of 174 hand-labelled WITH-AI records (42.5%) fit no entry. Design: the sample took every record the three LLM pre-labellers disagreed on (105 of 174) but only 6–9% of the other rows; 62 of the 74 are such rows, and no entry runs 59% / 22% / 10% across the disagree / two agree / three agree tiers. Substance: every rubric entry requires an LLM mechanism, which deepfake fraud and abuse imagery lack: the 148 deepfake records among the 174 split 61 no entry / 42 Misinformation / 39 Weaponized LLM Abuse / 6 other. The share describes this sample only.

## Use cases

- *Deployers of agents and copilots.* Improper Output Handling (corpus rank 2, vote 13) and MCP Tool Interface Exploitation (17 hand labels, no code) stand out in ON-AI; ATLAS names no control for either: the first maps to Command and Scripting Interpreter (0 ATLAS mitigations; ATT&CK T1059 has them), the second has no lookup technique; ATLAS's agent controls (human in the loop, tool and permission limits) are linked to 20% of records (Tables 5.1, 5.5, 5.6). Caveat: keyword labels, kept on 26% of Improper Output Handling rows.
- *Researchers.* Data and Model Poisoning: most-used ON-AI hand label (22), best-kept code (62%), flagged 'agree'; Improper Output Handling: realized, rarely demonstrated (5%) (Table 5.1). Caveat: 'demonstrated' tracks the research channel (3% of the corpus).
- *Framework authors.* 74 of 174 hand-labelled WITH-AI records fit no entry; Weaponized LLM Abuse draws 43 hand labels, no code (Tables 5.2, 5.3): the list's LLM-mechanism scope places deepfake fraud. Caveat: one coder, a quota sample.
- *Database maintainers.* MCP Tool Interface Exploitation and Weaponized LLM Abuse need a code; the Misinformation → Publish Poisoned Models link yields 1,314 unsound labels (Tables 5.1, 5.2, 5.A1). Caveat: labels are rules, nothing is coded yet.

**Not supported:** prevalence; a corrected Top 10; attack chains; mitigation adoption or efficacy; trends; attribution or exploitation status; technique-level statements about how attackers operated.
